# Devlog

## Day 1: Project setup

**What I did:** Installed Git and Python 3.13, created the GitHub repo, copied it to my PC, and set up the project folders (data, src, eval, docs). Then downloaded the EU AI Act and GDPR from EUR-Lex as PDFs and saved them in the data folder, with a sources note.

**Why:** I wanted one place for all my code and notes that I can pick up from any computer, and that also shows my progress step by step. The whole project runs on these two laws, so I wanted the official source and a record of which version I used.

**What I learned:** Git and GitHub keep my work backed up and in sync between machines. Keeping secrets like API keys out of GitHub from the start is much easier than fixing it later. Laws get amended, so I checked the date on each page and used the up-to-date text (the AI Act has a newer consolidated version from July 2026). I also checked that I'm allowed to reuse these documents and noted the credit.

**Checked the PDFs:** Wrote a small script that reads both PDFs. AI Act is 151 pages and GDPR is 88, and both came out as clean text with no broken characters. I noticed each page has a header line and that lines are wrapped mid-sentence, so I'll need to clean those up before splitting the text.

**Chunking results:** The script produced 641 chunks (300 from GDPR, 341 from the AI Act), with no missing article numbers and no leftover page headers. The AI Act has 119 articles instead of 113, and 14 annexes instead of 13, because the 2026 consolidated version includes new articles like 4a and 75a to 75d. A few things I noticed but left alone until I can measure them: some very short chunks and words split across lines by the PDF.

**First working search:** Turned all 641 chunks into embeddings with a small model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) and built a search that returns the 3 closest articles. Tried 6 questions by hand: for the 5 real questions the right article was in the top 3 every time (rank 1 in four of them, rank 2 for the AI Act fines question), and the off-topic pizza question scored only 0.09. Things I noticed: 291 of 641 chunks are longer than the model can read, so their endings are ignored. The lower-ranked results are often unrelated, and recitals show up a lot. The amendment markers (▼B, ▼M1) from the consolidated text show up in chunks and need cleaning.

**Answer key check:** Wrote 33 test questions (27 with a known correct article, 6 that shouldn't match anything). Before running any scores, I printed each expected article's opening to check it. One was wrong: I had listed Article 4a as relevant to AI training, but it's about bias detection data, so I removed it. Checking the key first matters, because a wrong answer in it would quietly ruin every score.

**Baseline scores:** Ran my search on 33 test questions. Right article in the top 3 for 67% (44% at rank 1), with an average ranking score of 0.55. It did best on questions worded like the law (80%) and worst on questions needing several articles (50%). Biggest problem: the explanatory recitals often rank above the actual articles, 4 of the 9 misses. Another: some AI Act questions pull neighbouring articles with high scores, so a high score doesn't mean a correct answer. My test set is small, so I won't read much into changes of one question.

**Experiment 1: leave out the recitals.** I predicted Hit@3 would rise from 67% to about 75-80%. It went to 74% (Hit@1 44% to 56%, Hit@5 70% to 85%, average ranking score 0.55 to 0.66). Nothing that worked before broke. The recitals explain the law but aren't binding, and this tool cites articles, so it makes sense to search only articles and annexes. What it didn't fix: everyday-language questions still miss (like "can I move my data to another service?"), and the AI Act's high-risk articles get confused with each other. My test set is small, so I'm treating a one-question change as noise.

**Cutoff test:** After switching models, my old "I don't know" cutoff stopped working because the new model gives higher scores in general. Re-testing, a cutoff of 0.55 keeps all my real questions and refuses 4 of 6 off-topic ones. A stricter 0.6 refuses 5 of 6 but also wrongly refuses 3 real questions. I'll use 0.55 for now, but only 6 of my questions are off-topic, so I'll add more before trusting it.

**Experiment 3: a re-ranker.** After the search finds 20 candidates, a second model (BGE reranker) reads the question together with each passage and re-orders them. Result: it made things worse. Hit@1 fell from 78% to 63% and the average ranking score from 0.81 to 0.73. It fixed one question but pushed the right answer out of the top 3 on two others that the plain search had ranked first. The right article is in the top 20 for 96% of questions, so the search finds almost everything and the re-ranker's ordering is the weak point (it was probably trained on general web text, not legal articles). I'm keeping the plain search and treating this as a negative result.

**Bigger answer key:** Grew the test set from 33 to 66 questions (52 with a known answer, 14 that shouldn't match, including near-misses like other countries' privacy laws). I set aside 11 as a held-out group that I only look at in totals, so I can't tune to them. Checked every new expected article against the text before scoring. Re-scored my three main setups.

**Re-ranker on the bigger test:** Re-ran it on 44 questions plus my held-out set. Same result as before: it's slightly worse than plain search at picking the right article first (64% vs 70%), and it again pushed out the right answer on questions where the plain search had it at rank 1. Its confidence scores are unreliable (0.999 for a wrong article). Three questions have the right article outside the top 20 entirely, so the problem is in the search and the chunks, not the ordering. I'm keeping the plain search.

**Experiment C, chunk size:** Tried 800, 1,500 and 2,000 characters. All three landed within one question of each other (84% to 86% right article in the top 3), so chunk size doesn't matter much for this data. I kept 1,500. With 2,000 characters only 1 of 495 chunks is longer than the model can read, compared with 291 of 641 for my first small model. The same six questions keep failing at every size, and they're all cases where I use everyday words and the law uses legal terms.

**Dashboard:** Built a one-page dashboard (Streamlit) that reads my saved result files. It shows every experiment in a table and chart, the results by question type, the "I don't know" cutoff curve, and the questions it still gets wrong

Results on 66 questions: when the right article reached the model it cited it every time (37 of 37 on my development questions), all 11 off-topic questions were refused (6 by the score cutoff, and 5 near-misses like HIPAA by the model itself), and one answer cited an article it wasn't given, which my code caught. The 5 real questions it refused were all ones where search missed the right article, so it said "I don't know" instead of guessing.

**Reading answers by hand:** Checked three answers against the article text. The breach-notification answer was accurate but left out the exception that you don't have to notify if the breach is unlikely to risk people's rights. Two others (non-EU companies, showing compliance) quoted their sources correctly but answered a different question than the one asked, because the right article never reached the model. Those are the worst kind of mistake, since they look well-sourced, and my citation check can't catch them: it only checks that the cited article was one of the passages given. The flagged citation was a different kind of slip: the model described Article 74 correctly but called it Article 54.

**Experiment 4, citing source numbers:** The model had mislabelled an article, so I changed the prompt to cite the numbered sources it was given (S1 to S5) and made my code turn those into article names. I predicted invalid citations would go from 1 to 0 with everything else within a question or two. That's what happened. One question (non-EU companies) flipped from a confident off-target answer to a refusal, which is the behaviour I want, but it's one question so I'm not claiming the prompt change caused it. It also showed that my "wrongly refused" number was misleading: all 6 refusals were questions where search never found the right article, so refusing was correct. When the right article reached the model, it answered and cited it every time (37 of 37); when it didn't, the model refused in 6 of 7 cases.

**Checking my own result:** The question-rewriting prompt had a few example legal words that I'd picked after seeing which questions failed, so I tested a version with no example words. Result: the right article in the top 3 for 93% either way (before rewriting it was 84%), and first for 89% vs 91%. So the gain comes from the idea, and I'm using the plain prompt. My held-out set was at 100% before and after, so it can't confirm the gain. I'll test with brand-new questions later.

**Experiment 6, answers with rewriting:** Connected the improved search to the answer step. I predicted about 40 to 41 of 44 questions would be answered with the right article cited. Result: 41 of 44, up from 37. All 11 off-topic questions are still refused and there are no invalid citations. One new thing: a question was refused even though the right article was in the passages, which I haven't yet looked into.
